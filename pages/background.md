## Motivation

Modern image generators produce faces that most people cannot tell apart from photographs.

{% include figure.html src="/docs/figures/faces/real_vs_fake.png" alt="Four real face photographs in the top row and four generated faces in the bottom row" caption="**Figure 1.** Top: real photographs from the FFHQ face dataset. Bottom: faces generated by StyleGAN. Both rows come from our training data and were picked by a fixed random seed, not chosen to look different." %}

A generator can still leave traces that are hard to see but easy to measure. Prior work
found that generators built from convolutional neural networks often fail to reproduce the
frequency content of real photographs, mainly because of the layers that enlarge images step
by step during generation [[2]](references.md). Instead of looking for one specific
frequency, we measure how image energy is spread across frequencies, directions, and scales,
and test whether that spread differs between real and generated images.

We use three digital signal processing (DSP) transforms:

- **Fast Fourier Transform (FFT):** how much energy the whole image has at each spatial frequency.
- **Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT):** the same idea with real cosine waves; this is also the basis JPEG compression uses.
- **Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT):** detail separated by direction (horizontal, vertical, diagonal) and by scale (fine or coarse).

These transforms turn each image into a small set of numbers (features). A classifier then
tests whether the features are actually useful for detection.

## Signal processing vs. machine learning

**Signal processing** lets us look directly at the frequency content of images. The features
are easy to interpret and to plot, and they need little data or computation. But a transform
is not a decision: something still has to turn the features into "real" or "fake", and
hand-picked thresholds can be fragile under noise, compression, or resizing.

**Machine learning** can learn how to combine many features instead of relying on one
threshold. It has its own risks: it needs labeled data and a clean split between training and
test images, it can pick up shortcuts (such as differences in compression) instead of real
differences, and it may fail on images from a generator it has never seen.

**Our approach** uses machine learning as the decision step on top of interpretable signal
processing features, not as a replacement for them. We start with a simple classifier
(Logistic Regression) and only then try more complex ones.
